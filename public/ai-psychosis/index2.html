Breaking development Federal court bars defendant from using AI

After federal prosecutors characterized my use of ChatGPT and Claude as evidence of “AI-induced psychosis,” the court has now ordered me not to use generative AI. This page was prepared before the restriction took effect and is being published through a prearranged release process.

United States v. Matthew Channon · 1:26-cr-02056-MLG · District of New Mexico

Federal prosecutors invoke “AI-induced psychosis” in a criminal competency request

Federal prosecutors are using a defendant’s reliance on ChatGPT and Claude as evidence of “AI-induced psychosis” and seeking a competency process that can result in psychiatric custody for up to 30 days, despite current court-ordered counseling records documenting intact functioning and no contrary clinical indications of risk.

Who I am

I’m a materials engineer and technology entrepreneur, and I ran for the U.S. Senate from New Mexico in 2026. My broader background is at mattchannon.com.

I began using ChatGPT, Claude, and other AI tools to investigate what I believe are serious authenticity problems in records from my federal cases.

I used them to compare filings, examine document metadata and imagery, and research federal procedure.

Now the government is using its claim of “AI-induced psychosis” to argue that I am too mentally ill to defend myself.

Why this is newsworthy

This is not only an unusual phrase appearing in a federal filing. Prosecutors are using a defendant’s reliance on ChatGPT and Claude as part of an argument that he may be mentally unable to participate properly in his own defense.

At the same time, Channon is already under a court-ordered counseling regime, currently twice a month. The provider’s May 8 intake note — available here — records intact functioning and states: “Patient denies all areas of risk. No contrary clinical indications present.”

Prosecutors are nevertheless seeking a competency examination that can result in psychiatric custody for up to 30 days — potentially 45 with an extension. That creates a direct public-interest question about escalation, cost, and judicial oversight when the court’s existing treatment process is producing reassuring current findings.

The case therefore combines three developing issues in one federal prosecution: the use of generative-AI activity as evidence of mental incompetence, the possibility of psychiatric confinement despite contrary current treatment evidence, and the public use of highly sensitive mental-health material in a criminal docket.

A first-of-its-kind claim of “AI-induced psychosis”

Until now, legal cases involving “AI-induced psychosis” have run in the opposite direction: users and families have sued chatbot companies, alleging that AI caused, reinforced, or worsened psychiatric harm.

In United States v. Channon, the government has inverted that theory. Federal prosecutors are using a claim of “AI-induced psychosis” against the chatbot user himself, arguing that his reliance on ChatGPT and Claude is evidence that he is too mentally ill to defend himself.

I have found no prior reported criminal case in which a prosecutor used “AI-induced psychosis” this way.

The AI involvement predates the prosecution

Before the April 1 transaction that led to the current charge, I genuinely did not know whether the federal government considered me a prohibited person. I had received conflicting signals about my status and had no realistic way to force a clear answer.

I used Claude to work through how to obtain an answer from the live NICS system without ultimately taking possession of a firearm. Claude discussed the Form 4473 issues with me, how to structure the test, and even what type of firearm to select.

The purpose was to get a straight answer from the government — and, if the result was a denial, to create the transaction required to lodge a formal NICS denial challenge. I also wanted the test to occur in a setting where, if the process were interfered with, that interference would have to become visible.

The uncertainty was not abstract. A law-enforcement background report later produced in discovery identified three possible criminal records, but did not list the federal felony conviction the government now says I knowingly lied about. View the government’s own redacted four-page zero-felony-history excerpt.

That April 1 transaction became the basis of the federal charge. The government is now citing my reliance on AI as evidence of “AI-induced psychosis.”

How the mental-health record was built

The government’s competency argument did not begin with the September AI filing. Earlier in the case, prosecutors relied on U.S. Marshals Service reports describing prior psychiatric hospitalization, alleged diagnoses, medications, statements attributed to Channon, and statements from family members.

Those reports were filed on the public docket as exhibits to the government’s earlier motion seeking access to a court-ordered mental-health assessment. The later AI filing then incorporated that earlier material into the renewed competency argument.

The publicly filed reports contain a psychiatric diagnosis, specific prescription medications, statements attributed to Channon during hospitalization, and third-party family contact information, including a home address and phone number. That material was later reused in proceedings capable of affecting his liberty.
